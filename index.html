The science

Sound from steel.

A thin metal plate rings at frequencies set by its shape. Resonance is designed to use that: no power, no radio, just a struck disk with a pattern cut into its edge.

01 · Strike

A pin pull releases a striker.

Removing the pin is designed to let a small arm flick the disk once. The strike is mechanical, so it needs no battery.

02 · Ring

The disk vibrates in its own modes.

A struck plate rings at natural bending modes. For a plain disk, frequency rises with thickness and falls with the square of its diameter, and it depends on the metal's stiffness and density.

f ∝ (t / d²) · √(E / ρ)
03 · Fingerprint

Notches shift the peaks.

Cutouts along the rim change local stiffness and mass, moving the modes. Each notch pattern gives a different set of frequency peaks. The pattern, not the loudness, is the code.

04 · Listen

A nearby microphone reads the peaks.

A listener samples fast enough to capture the tag's band, finds the strongest peaks and matches them to known codes. The research did this with simple rules and no machine learning.

The research behind it

Resonance builds on SoundOff.

“SoundOff: Low-cost Passive Ultrasound Tags for Non-invasive and Non-Intrusive Smart Home Sensing” by Yibo Fu, Bolei Deng, Alexander Adams and Josiah Hester at Georgia Tech, published in Proceedings of the ACM on Interactive, Mobile, Wearable and Ubiquitous Technologies (2025). Read the paper.

>98%reported identification accuracy within 1 m
~1,300candidate disk designs found in simulation
15disks the team physically tested
<4¢reported cost of one laser-cut disk

What the paper shows

  • A 14 mm, 1 mm thick stainless steel disk, laser cut with a center hole and edge cutouts, rings above 20 kHz when flicked by a printed cantilever on a door or drawer.
  • Disks tuned to different frequencies can be told apart by a microphone with simple signal processing.
  • Accuracy fell below 50% at 1.5 m and failed at 3 m, so the signal stays local.

What Resonance changes

  • The trigger: a retail tag pin pull instead of a door or drawer.
  • The disk: our CAD uses a 9.5 mm, 0.254 mm stainless shim in a swappable cartridge.
  • An added 58 kHz label so the tag can also meet existing AM exit gates.

What we have not proven

  • That our thinner, smaller disks give distinct, repeatable peaks.
  • That the lowest peaks sit above hearing range. An ideal-disk estimate puts one near 14 kHz, which is audible.
  • That a listener copes with store noise. Our tests are planned, not run.

The research reports results for door and drawer sensing. None of its numbers are results for Resonance.

    Notched steel disk≈ $0.04 or less

    Reported for SoundOff's laser-cut 316 stainless disks, about 500 per 0.3 × 0.3 m sheet. Our 301 stainless shim has not been quoted.

    58 kHz AM exit label≈ $0.01 – $0.04

    From marketplace listings, Nov 2026: $0.01 each at a 500,000-piece minimum, about $0.04 each in a 5,000-label pack. Not a distributor quote.

    Body, striker, cartridge, pinNot yet priced

    A reusable multi-part tag has more to make than a disposable label. This is the largest unknown and decides whether the tag can compete.

    Shelf listenerHardware not chosen

    The research prototype used a Raspberry Pi Zero (about 0.8 W) and a 384 kHz USB ultrasonic microphone. We have not picked ours.

    Why reuse matters

    Cost per use is tag cost divided by the number of times it is used. A tag that costs more than a disposable one can still work if it is reused enough. We have not measured how many cycles the pin, striker and disk survive.

    cost per use = tag cost ÷ reuses
